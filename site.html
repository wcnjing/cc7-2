The problem

Singapore is ageing, and today's wearables aren't built for it

~1 in 4

Nearly 1 in 4 Singapore citizens will be 65+ by 2030.

Ministry of Health (2026); National Population and Talent Division, Population in Brief.

35%

35% of older adults (23 of 65) did not intend to keep using a fitness tracker after a 7-day trial; usability mattered.

Muñoz Esquivel et al. (2023), JMIR Aging.

Even with an accessible app and community support, complex navigation still challenged some seniors.

Bao et al. (2025), JMIR Formative Research.

How it works

A watch first, a health tracker second

Everything a senior already knows about wearing a watch still works. The health features sit quietly underneath.

Analogue hands

Reads like a normal watch. No learning needed to tell the time.

Physical dial

Turn to switch readings, press to select. No swiping or tapping.

One reading at a time

A memory-in-pixel screen shows one large, high-contrast number.

Stretch-on fabric strap

Slides over the hand. No buckle or clasp to fasten.

Heart-rate & activity tracking
Fall sensing with emergency call
Audible reminders
About one-week battery (design target)

Who it's for

Designed with four groups in mind

Seniors

Wear a familiar watch and check health without learning an app.

Caregivers

Get alerts for falls and missed reminders without daily check-in calls.

Active Ageing Centres

Support members with a simple device staff can explain in minutes.

Device manufacturers

Reach an underserved, growing market with a proven watch form.

Our approach

Built with three lenses

STEM

Can it work?

A low-power memory-in-pixel screen and simple sensors aim for about a week per charge, while fall detection and heart-rate tracking run quietly in the background.

Business / Governance

Can people afford it?

Two pricing options and a path to subsidy under the Seniors' Mobility and Enabling Fund aim to keep costs within reach for seniors and families.

Social Sciences / Humanities

Will people want it?

A familiar watch avoids the stigma of "medical" devices, and seniors stay in control of what is shared with family.

Together: a watch seniors can operate, afford, and choose to use.

Concerns we're addressing

What we still need to prove

We will run hands-on sessions with seniors, including those with arthritis or low vision, to test whether they can put on the strap and use the dial unaided.

Fall alerts include a 10-second "Are you OK?" check to reduce false alarms. We will measure false-alarm and missed-fall rates before any real-world use.

The one-week battery is a design target. We will check it against existing hybrid-watch components and adjust features if needed.

Prices are team estimates. We will test them with families and explore subsidy eligibility so cost isn't a barrier.

Seniors choose who sees their data, and location is shared only during an emergency alert. We will ask seniors directly what they are comfortable sharing.
